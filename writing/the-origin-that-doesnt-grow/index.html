Your origin doesn't get busier as your audience grows to millions

Centralized live video sends every viewer their own copy from a server you pay for, so cost tracks the audience exactly. Herald's origin doesn't: at one hundred thousand viewers, 99.9% of the stream already came from each other.

September 2026

Twitch and YouTube send live video from a server farm sized to the audience, because every viewer gets their own copy of every second. Grow from a thousand viewers to two million and you need two thousand times the bandwidth, because the origin is the only thing sending.

Herald's viewers send to each other instead. The video below grows one from a handful of viewers to two million and shows exactly what the origin sends the whole time.

One stream, growing from a handful of viewers to two million.

What the origin actually sends

At one hundred thousand viewers pulling a stream, the origin sends 129 copies of each segment, not one hundred thousand. Ninety nine point nine percent of the audience is already getting it from someone else watching.

A viewer that hears about a new segment from another viewer waits three seconds for the swarm to deliver it before asking the origin at all, because a segment a neighbor already has is one the swarm probably already has too. At twenty thousand viewers that turns 130 origin sends a segment into 26: a 0.13% share of the audience.

Carry that share out to two million and the origin sits at about 2,600 sends a segment: five gigabits a second. The naive version, one copy per viewer, needs four terabits at that size. Roughly seven hundred times less.

The flat line at the end

The video also shows a harder ceiling: an operator can pin the origin at exactly eight sends a segment, sixteen megabits, no matter how large the audience gets past that. The share curve already does most of the work on its own; the ceiling is there for whoever wants a hard number instead of a shrinking percentage.

What sets it apart

Every live platform at real scale hits the same wall: one server can't send a copy to every viewer once the audience gets big, so you either build a CDN sized to your peak or find another way to move the bytes. Herald's bet is that the audience already is the delivery network.
